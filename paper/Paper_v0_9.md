# Abstract

Multiobjective team assignment in home health care must cope with uncertain affinities between patients and care teams. We ask whether aggregating population information with Dempster–Shafer evidence, rather than with a probabilistic frequency model, improves a quantum-inspired genetic algorithm, which can be read as a univariate model-based search. We report a sequence of studies whose contrasts, equivalence margins and decision rules were fixed before each run, using paired seeds, an oracle hypervolume measure and semi-synthetic instances built on benchmark geographies, with simulated hidden truth and evidence. The evidential target clearly outperformed the simple probabilistic target, but a probabilistic target sharpened by a power transformation did at least as well on the main instances, and sharpening the evidential target further degraded its performance. Refinements of the evidential structure brought no robust gain, and guiding the mutation of a classical algorithm with the same targets did not help. On two new families of instances, the advantage over the simple probabilistic target was reproduced, whereas the ordering against the sharpened target was not stable. A final study gave the probabilistic target a concentration schedule calibrated to follow that of the evidential target: the evidential target was better when the source strength was low and worse when it was high, and the calibration controlled only two concentration diagnostics. Classical multiobjective algorithms remained far better than all variants of the quantum-inspired algorithm. No representation advantage independent of concentration was established.

**Keywords:** home health care; team assignment; multiobjective optimisation; quantum-inspired genetic algorithm; Dempster–Shafer theory; estimation of distribution; hypervolume; pre-registered experiments.

# 1. Introduction

Home health care (HHC) organisations assign patients to care teams under competing objectives: travel effort, patient-side quality of service, and workload balance with skill adequacy. The quality of a candidate assignment depends on quantities that are only partially known when the plan is built, such as the adequacy of a team for a skill or the satisfaction a team is expected to produce. Belief-function (Dempster–Shafer) models represent partial, imprecise and conflicting information, and can carry it into a search algorithm. Quantum-inspired evolutionary algorithms maintain, for each decision variable, a probability-like amplitude that is rotated towards good solutions; they can be read as model-based search schemes whose sampling distribution is parametrised by angles (Section 2.2).

This paper asks a narrow question. In one quantum-inspired genetic algorithm, QGA2-E, the amplitudes are rotated towards a *target distribution* built from the best individuals of the current population. The target can be built by a probabilistic frequency model, or by Dempster–Shafer evidence combined with Dempster's rule and converted to a probability by the pignistic transformation. We observed early on that the evidential target gives a better hypervolume than a simple probabilistic target. The question is why. Two explanations compete. The first is that the evidential *representation* carries something the probabilistic model does not. The second is that the evidential construction simply yields a *more concentrated* target, and that concentration, not representation, drives the gain. They make different predictions: if concentration is the mechanism, a probabilistic target sharpened by a power transformation should reproduce the gain, and sharpening the evidential target further should not help.

**Why study QGA2-E, which is not competitive?** QGA2-E keeps one angle vector per patient and rotates it towards a target; sampling is independent across patients, so the algorithm is a *univariate model-based search* in the family of estimation-of-distribution algorithms (EDAs), with a rotation update comparable to the probability-vector update of PBIL (Section 2.4). That structure makes the object of study explicit: the update direction is a single, inspectable distribution per patient, the *target*, which can be built in different ways and whose concentration can be measured and tempered. In a crossover-based algorithm the corresponding component (what bias a population imposes on new solutions) is spread over selection, recombination and mutation and cannot be isolated. We therefore use QGA2-E as a *testbed for target construction in model-based search*, not as a proposal to solve the home-care assignment problem: NSGA-II and MOEA/D, run at the same budget, are far better on every instance family (Section 6.2), and we make no claim of competitiveness. A plausible reason for the gap, which we did not test, is that a univariate model cannot represent dependences between the team choices of different patients, which crossover can exploit implicitly (Section 7); how to close the gap is outside the scope of this paper.

We formalise the two targets and their concentration (Section 4), prove that the evidential target accumulates agreement exponentially while the frequency target does so linearly, and show that the sharpening operation is a tempering with a monotone effect on entropy. We then answer the question with a sequence of pre-registered experiments rather than a single comparison. The contribution is methodological and empirical:

1. a controlled comparison of evidential and probabilistic targets in one algorithm, with paired seeds, an oracle hypervolume and explicit decision rules including an equivalence margin;
2. a symmetric sharpening operation applied to both representations, and a curve of performance against the sharpening exponent;
3. a series of refinements (dependence correction, ignorance as a selection objective, adaptive regulation, diversity) that tested whether the evidential structure could do more than a sharper target;
4. a transfer experiment that guides the mutation of a classical NSGA-II with the same targets;
5. a sweep of the dependence-degree parameter of the dependence-corrected target against concentration-matched probabilistic targets, and a local sensitivity study of the source strength, the similarity threshold and the sharpening exponent, with explicit reporting of when the concentration match failed;
6. two independent pre-registered evaluations on new families of instances (synthetic geographies, then geographies from public benchmark files), which test which earlier contrasts hold outside the set-up on which the decisions were made;
7. a study that replaces the fixed exponent of the sharpened probabilistic target by a concentration schedule calibrated to the trajectory of the evidential target, to separate representation from schedule (on two concentration diagnostics only);
8. a full account of what was and was not pre-registered, including declared incidents.

The conclusions are deliberately limited. The results are compatible with an important role for the concentration of the target; they do not demonstrate that concentration is the only mechanism, and they do not establish that the evidential representation can never help. NSGA-II and MOEA/D, run at the same evaluation budget, remain well above every QGA2-E variant, so the study concerns a representation *inside* QGA2-E and makes no claim of competitiveness.

Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 formalises the problem, the instances and the information model. Section 4 describes the algorithms and the targets with their mathematical properties. Section 5 describes the experimental design and statistical decision rules. Section 6 reports results, including the sequence of studies and the concentration-schedule study (Section 6.8). Sections 7 to 9 discuss, state limitations and conclude.

# 2. Related work

This section is based on targeted searches performed for this draft; it is not a systematic review and does not support any claim about what is new.

## 2.1 Home health care routing, scheduling and assignment

Three recent surveys structure the field. Fikar and Hirsch [1] review home health care routing and scheduling; Cissé et al. [2] review the operations-research problems related to HHC (routing, scheduling, staff assignment and their combinations); Di Mascolo et al. [3] combine a literature survey with a bibliometric analysis. Typical models combine time windows, skill matching, continuity of care and workload constraints. Bi-objective formulations exist: Braekers et al. [4] analyse the trade-off between costs and client inconvenience, and Rasmussen et al. [5] address crew scheduling with preference-based visit clustering and temporal dependencies. The formulation of this paper is simpler than integrated routing: it assigns patients to teams and evaluates three objectives for a fixed visit order (Section 3). That simplification isolates the question of how information about uncertain affinities enters the search, at the cost of external validity for integrated routing.

## 2.2 Quantum-inspired evolutionary algorithms

Han and Kim [6] introduced a quantum-inspired evolutionary algorithm (QIEA) for combinatorial optimisation, where each variable has a Q-bit whose observation yields a binary value, and a rotation gate moves the amplitudes towards the best solution found; a later version added a termination criterion, a different gate and a two-phase scheme [7]. Multiobjective extensions include a QIEA for multiobjective knapsack problems [8] and a framework with convergence conditions [9] (Li and Rudolph, technical report). In these algorithms a probability distribution over decisions is parametrised by angles; the update is a stochastic-approximation step towards a target. The QGA2 of this paper uses two amplitudes per team and a categorical observation (Section 4.1).

## 2.3 Dempster–Shafer theory

Dempster [10] introduced upper and lower probabilities induced by a multivalued mapping and the rule of combination later formalised by Shafer [11]. The transferable belief model of Smets and Kennes [12] separates credal and pignistic levels and defines the pignistic transformation used here to obtain a probability for decisions. Yager's variant [13] transfers the conflicting mass to the whole frame instead of renormalising; it is used for the evidence on team–skill adequacy (Section 3.4). Denœux [14] reviews decision-making with belief functions. Evidence theory has also been used to model epistemic uncertainty in design optimisation and reliability analysis [36–38] and, through the evidential reasoning approach, for multiple-attribute decision analysis [39, 40]; these works concern the evaluation of solutions or the ranking of alternatives. Their positioning relative to target aggregation in model-based search is summarised in Section 2.6.

## 2.4 Estimation of distribution algorithms and concentration

Estimation-of-distribution algorithms (EDAs) replace crossover and mutation by sampling from a probabilistic model estimated from selected individuals; Baluja's PBIL [15] keeps a probability vector updated towards the best individuals, and Hauschild and Pelikan [16] survey the field (see also the book of Larrañaga and Lozano [41]). A recurrent theme is the loss of diversity: Shapiro [17] shows that the finite sampling and selection of EDAs produce drift that drives the model to a degenerate distribution, with consequences for scalability. The *concentration* of the sampling distribution (its entropy or its maximum probability) is therefore a central control parameter of such algorithms. In Section 4.3 we show that the power transformation used to sharpen targets is a tempering whose inverse temperature is the exponent, which puts concentration on an explicit one-parameter scale. QGA2-E belongs to this family: it keeps a separate distribution for each patient (a univariate model) and moves it towards a target built from the non-dominated individuals, so the way the target is built, and how concentrated it becomes over time, is the part of the algorithm that decides how fast the model degenerates.

## 2.5 Reporting practice

The hypervolume indicator is the standard unary quality measure for multiobjective fronts [18][19]. Confidence intervals use the bootstrap [20]. Practical equivalence is assessed with two one-sided tests [21] or, equivalently, by checking that a confidence interval lies inside an equivalence margin [22]; multiplicity within a family uses Bonferroni or Holm [23] adjustments. Reproducibility practices for evolutionary computation are discussed by López-Ibáñez et al. [24]. Pre-registration of hypotheses before data collection is advocated in the empirical sciences [25]. NSGA-II [26] and MOEA/D [27] are used here as baselines.

## 2.6 Positioning

The question addressed is whether, when an evidential target outperforms a probabilistic one in a model-based search, the advantage comes from the representation or from the concentration it induces, and, more finely, whether it comes from the representation or from the *schedule* on which the target concentrates (Dempster's rule sharpens adaptively as agreement grows; a power transformation of a frequency model sharpens by a fixed amount). We study this in a univariate model-based search (an EDA-like algorithm), on a stylised home-care assignment problem with simulated information, with a design that sharpens both targets by the same operation, matches concentration where possible, and reports pre-registered contrasts, including inconclusive and negative ones. The contribution is a controlled comparison and a documented set of negative results about when the evidential representation does not help, not a new competitive algorithm. In the literature reviewed in Sections 2.2–2.4, belief functions in optimisation address uncertain objective evaluations or post-optimisation decision analysis [36–40], whereas estimation-of-distribution and quantum-inspired algorithms update their sampling models from probabilistic frequencies or amplitude vectors [6–9, 15–17, 41]. Within the scope of this targeted review, the present study provides a controlled comparison, with pre-specified decision rules, of evidential versus probabilistic target aggregation inside a univariate model-based search after matching concentration and annealing schedules.

# 3. Problem, instances and information model

## 3.1 Decision space

*Notation.* A *world* of adequacies and satisfactions is written $\xi=(A,S)$; the symbol $\omega=-\ln(1-w)$ is reserved for the weight of evidence of a source (Section 4.2).

Let $\mathcal P=\{1,\dots,n_p\}$ be the patients and $\mathcal T=\{1,\dots,N_T\}$ the $N_T$ teams. Each patient $p$ requires a set of skills $\mathrm{need}_p\subseteq\{1,\dots,5\}$; team $t$ is certified for a skill set given by a binary matrix $C\in\{0,1\}^{N_T\times 5}$. The formulation allows sets of skills; in the generated instances each patient requires exactly one skill ($|\mathrm{need}_p|=1$, Section 3.4 and Appendix B). The mask $M_{pt}=1$ iff $C_{tk}=1$ for all $k\in\mathrm{need}_p$, and $\mathcal A_p=\{t:M_{pt}=1\}$ is the set of allowed teams, of size $K_p$. A solution is an assignment $x\in\prod_p\mathcal A_p$. Each team visits its patients in increasing patient index (fixed visit order); the visit sequence is not optimised, so the problem is assignment with a fixed order, not integrated routing. Team 0 is an ordinary team except in instance generation, where it is used as a technical device (Section 3.4).

## 3.2 Schedule and objectives

For team $t$ let $\mathcal V_t=\{p_1<\dots<p_m\}$ be its patients, $D$ the travel-time matrix with depot $0$, $[e_p,l_p]$ the time window of $p$, $v$ the visit duration and $T_{\mathrm{end}}$ the day end. Arrival at $p_j$ is
$$a_{p_j}=\max\{e_{p_j},\;c_{j-1}+D(p_{j-1},p_j)\},\qquad c_j=a_{p_j}+v,$$
with $c_0=0$ and $p_0$ the depot. The route length is $rl_t=\sum_j D(p_{j-1},p_j)+D(p_m,0)$ (waiting is not counted). The three objectives, all in $[0,1]$ and all minimised, are
$$f_1=\min\Big\{1,\frac{\sum_t rl_t}{T_{\mathrm{end}}\,N_T}\Big\},$$
$$f_2=1-\frac1{n_p}\sum_{p}\Big[0.7\big(0.5\,tw_p+0.3\,pref_p+0.2\,punct_p\big)+0.3\,S(x_p,g_p)\Big],$$
$$f_3=1-\mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]}\Big(0.3\max\{0,bal\}+0.7\,\frac1{n_p}\sum_p\min_{k\in\mathrm{need}_p}A(x_p,k)-0.2\,over\Big),$$
where

- $tw_p=1$ if $e_p\le a_p\le l_p$, and otherwise $tw_p=\max\{0,\,1-|a_p-\tfrac{e_p+l_p}{2}|/(l_p-e_p+10^{-9})\}$. Because arrivals before $e_p$ are replaced by waiting, only late arrivals ($a_p>l_p$) reach the second branch, where it equals $\max\{0,\,\tfrac12-(a_p-l_p)/(l_p-e_p)\}$. The function therefore **jumps from 1 to about 0.5 when a window is missed**; this is the implemented behaviour and is kept as is. **Interpretation.** We read the jump as a service-level step: meeting the window is rewarded in full, while missing it is a breach that removes about half of the satisfaction, after which the value decays linearly with the lateness measured relative to the window length (it reaches zero when the lateness equals half the window length). The step is a modelling choice made for the original experiments and kept unchanged here so that all pre-registered results remain valid; the results are therefore conditional on this objective, and a continuous penalty was not evaluated (Section 8). The implementation guards the denominator by $10^{-9}$; the instance generator guarantees $l_p>e_p$ (it asserts it when drawing each window; checked in the code).
- $pref_p=1$ if $p$ has no preferred team or $x_p$ is the preferred team, and $0.10$ otherwise;
- $punct_p=\max\{0,\,1-|a_p-PT_p|/(T_{\mathrm{end}}+1)\}$ for a target arrival time $PT_p$;
- $S(t,g)$ is the satisfaction of team $t$ for the care type $g_p$ of patient $p$ (the group of its lowest-numbered required skill; skills are grouped as $\{0,1\},\{2,3\},\{4\}$);
- $A(t,k)$ is the adequacy of team $t$ for skill $k$;
- $bal=1-\mathrm{std}(rl)/(\mathrm{mean}(rl)+10^{-9})$ is the workload balance (it can be negative, hence the $\max$) and $over$ is the share of teams with $rl_t>MR$. The overload penalty is subtracted *inside* the clipping, as written.


The objective vector is $f(x;\xi)=(f_1,f_2,f_3)$ and depends on a *world* $\xi=(A,S)$ of adequacies and satisfactions. $f_1$ does not depend on the world.

## 3.3 Quality measure

For a finite set $F\subset[0,1]^3$ and the reference point $r=(1.2,1.2,1.2)$, the hypervolume is the Lebesgue measure of the region dominated by $F$ and bounded by $r$:
$$\mathrm{HV}(F)=\lambda\Big(\bigcup_{f\in F}[f,r]\Big),$$
computed exactly. For a run, let $\mathcal X$ be the archive of all solutions evaluated during the search that are non-dominated *with respect to the nominal objectives* $f(\cdot;\hat\xi)$ (the archive is updated after each evaluation and the oracle values play no part in it). The **oracle hypervolume** is $\mathrm{HV}\big(\{f(x;\xi^\ast):x\in\mathcal X\}\big)$, where $\xi^\ast$ is the hidden true world. It is therefore the hypervolume of the *nominally selected* archive after a single oracle re-evaluation, not of the set of solutions that are non-dominated in the oracle world among all those evaluated; the search itself only evaluates in a *nominal* world $\hat\xi$ (Section 3.4). The oracle world enters only in this last re-evaluation, a rule that was tested before every confirmatory run.

## 3.4 Instances and information model

**Instances.** Six scenarios derive from the Solomon benchmark [28]: S4 (30 patients, 8 teams) and S5 (50 patients, 10 teams) crossed with the topologies RC101, R101 and C101 (Appendix B). Only the customer coordinates come from the Solomon instance (the depot and the first 30 or 50 customers); for each scenario and seed an instance is generated: time windows, certification matrix $C$ (with a repair step: if some patient cannot be served by any team, team 0 is given all the skills that patient requires, with random skill levels, so that every instance is feasible; team 0 is otherwise an ordinary team, but the repair raises its coverage and can shape assignment patterns in the instances where it is triggered), and hidden truth.

**Hidden truth** (as generated). A fraction 25% of certified cells is flagged *unverified*, and 35% of those are in truth not competent (hidden failure, $C^{\mathrm{true}}_{tk}=0$). Let $Q_{tk}=\text{level}_{tk}/2$ for a skill level in $\{0,1,2\}$ and $E_{tk}=\mathrm{clip}(0.15+0.6Q_{tk}+\epsilon,0,1)$ with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,0.15^2)$. The true adequacy is $A^\ast_{tk}=C^{\mathrm{true}}_{tk}(0.6Q_{tk}+0.4E_{tk})$ and the true satisfaction is $S^\ast_{tg}=\mathrm{clip}(0.35+0.45\bar A_{tg}+\eta,\,0.02,\,0.98)$ with $\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,0.10^2)$ and $\bar A_{tg}$ the mean true adequacy over the certified cells of group $g$. (The constants were checked against the generator code.)

**Evidence on adequacy.** For each certified cell $(t,k)$ the frame is $\Theta_A=\{a,\bar a\}$ (adequate, not adequate). Three sources, generated from separate pseudo-random streams and therefore independent conditionally on the hidden truth (their errors are not correlated beyond the common truth $A^\ast$), provide mass functions $m(\{a\}),m(\{\bar a\}),m(\Theta_A)$:

- *Document* (covering a random 75% of the certified, non-flagged cells): $(0.5,\,0,\,0.5)$ if covered, and the vacuous $(0,0,1)$ otherwise (also for flagged and non-certified cells).
- *Supervision*: a noisy score $\hat y=\mathrm{clip}(A^\ast+\mathcal N(0,0.25^2),0,1)$ and $s=|2\hat y-1|$, giving $(s,0,1-s)$ if $\hat y\ge 0.5$ and $(0,s,1-s)$ otherwise.
- *History*: $n\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(2)$ past observations, $x\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,A^\ast)$ successes, and masses $\big(\tfrac{x}{n+2},\tfrac{n-x}{n+2},\tfrac{2}{n+2}\big)$.

Each source $i$ is discounted by a reliability $r_i$ (Shafer's rule): ${}^{r}m(B)=r\,m(B)$ for $B\in\{\{a\},\{\bar a\}\}$ and ${}^{r}m(\Theta_A)=1-r+r\,m(\Theta_A)$, with $r=0.95$ (document), $0.80$ (supervision) and $1.0$ (history). The discounted masses are combined by the conjunctive rule $m_{12}(B)=\sum_{C\cap D=B}m_1(C)m_2(D)$; the conflict $\kappa=m_{12}(\emptyset)$ is recorded and transferred to the frame (Yager), $m(\Theta_A)\leftarrow m(\Theta_A)+\kappa$. The nominal adequacy of a certified cell is
$$\hat A_{tk}=0.25+0.60\,\mathrm{BetP}(a),\qquad \mathrm{BetP}(a)=m(\{a\})+\tfrac12 m(\Theta_A),$$
and $\hat A_{tk}=0$ on non-certified cells.

**Evidence on satisfaction.** For each team and group, $v\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(2)$ votes with $x\sim\mathrm{Bin}(v,S^\ast)$ positive votes give the masses $\big(\tfrac{x}{v+2},\tfrac{v-x}{v+2},\tfrac2{v+2}\big)$ and the nominal satisfaction $\hat S=\tfrac{x}{v+2}+\tfrac1{v+2}$ (its pignistic probability). The nominal world is $\hat\xi=(\hat A,\hat S)$. The optimiser uses these point estimates as if they were the true values: the masses, the ignorance and the conflict are *not* propagated into the objectives $f_1$ to $f_3$. The independence of the evidence sources in this paragraph is distinct from the dependence between *individuals* of the population that is discussed for the target in Section 4.2.

**Comparison worlds.** Two further worlds appear only in the early comparisons of Section 6.3: a *non-informative world* ($\hat A=0.55$ on certified cells, $\hat S=0.5$), and a *probabilistic-average world*, where $\hat A=0.25+0.60\cdot\tfrac13\sum_i \mathrm{BetP}_i(a)$ averages the pignistic probabilities of the three discounted sources without Dempster combination and $\hat S$ is as in the nominal world. The main experiments of Sections 6.4 to 6.6 all use the nominal world.

**Budget.** Every algorithm in a comparison uses a population of 40 and 150 generations, i.e. $40+150\times40=6{,}040$ evaluations (MOEA/D uses 6,030, declared).

# 4. Algorithms and target models

## 4.1 QGA2-E

**State.** The population has $\mu=40$ individuals. Each individual $i$ carries its own angle array $\theta^{(i)}\in[0,\pi/2]^{n_p\times N_T}$ (entries outside the mask $M$ are zero) and the assignment $x^{(i)}\in\prod_p\mathcal A_p$ obtained from it. Initial angles are $\arcsin\sqrt{1/N_T}$ plus Gaussian noise ($\sigma=0.02$), clipped to $[0,\pi/2]$ and masked.

**Decoding (observation with collapse).** For individual $i$ and patient $p$, let $\pi_{pt}=\sin^2\theta^{(i)}_{pt}\,M_{pt}$. A Bernoulli measurement $z_{pt}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi_{pt})$ is drawn for each allowed team. If at least one team is measured as 1, the team is drawn among the measured ones with probabilities $\pi_{pt}z_{pt}/\sum_s\pi_{ps}z_{ps}$; otherwise it is drawn among the allowed teams with probabilities $\pi_{pt}/\sum_s\pi_{ps}$; if all $\pi_{pt}$ vanish, it is drawn uniformly on $\mathcal A_p$. A forbidden team is never returned. The normalisation $\pi_{pt}/\sum_s\pi_{ps}$ therefore describes only the second branch.

**Generation.** At generation $g$, (i) the current population consists of the survivors of the previous selection (at $g=0$, after a first selection on the initial population); (ii) the source set $S$ is the first non-dominated front of this population on the three nominal objectives and the target $q$ is computed from it *before any child of the generation exists*; (iii) every individual's angles are rotated towards $q$ (below) and a child is decoded from the rotated angles, the child keeping them; (iv) the 40 children are evaluated; (v) the 40 parents and 40 children, each with its angles, are pooled and the best 40 by non-dominated sorting and crowding distance survive (the "E" for elitist).

In the original rotation, child $i$ moves towards the single best individual. Here the rotation is directed to a *target distribution* $q(p,\cdot)$ computed once per generation from the *source set* $S$ (step ii above) and shared by all individuals:
$$\theta^{(i)\prime}_{p\cdot}=\mathrm{clip}\Big(\theta^{(i)}_{p\cdot}+\delta\,\big(q_{p\cdot}-e_{x^{(i)}(p)}\big),\,0,\,\tfrac\pi2\Big)\odot M_{p\cdot},\qquad \delta=0.05\pi,$$
where $e_x$ is the one-hot vector of team $x$ and $x^{(i)}(p)$ is the team currently assigned to $p$ by individual $i$. When the target is the one-hot vector of a single source (the best individual), the update reduces exactly to the original rotation; this reduction was a blocking test.

## 4.2 Targets

Fix a patient $p$ with $K=K_p$ allowed teams (patients with $K_p=1$ are not mutable; their target is trivially $q=1$ on the single team, the singleton coincides with the frame, and they are excluded from the concentration statistics). Let $N=|S|$ be the number of sources, $c_t$ the number of sources assigning team $t$ ($\sum_t c_t=N$), $w=0.3$ the support strength and $\varepsilon=0.02$ a probability floor.

**Dp (probabilistic).** $q(t)=w\,c_t/N+(1-w)/K$, the mean over sources of the pignistic distribution of a source that supports its team with mass $w$ (the remaining $1-w$ spread uniformly).

**E (evidential).** Each source $i$ is a simple support function: mass $w$ on $\{x_i(p)\}$ and $1-w$ on the frame $\Theta_p=\mathcal A_p$. Dempster's rule combines the $N$ sources; the target is the pignistic probability $q(t)=m(\{t\})+m(\Theta_p)/K$.

> **Proposition 1 (closed form).** With $\omega=-\ln(1-w)$ and $D=1+\sum_{t}(e^{\omega c_t}-1)$, Dempster's combination of the $N$ simple supports gives $m(\{t\})=(e^{\omega c_t}-1)/D$ and $m(\Theta_p)=1/D$.
>
> *Proof.* Put $u_t=(1-w)^{c_t}$. The $c_t$ identical supports of team $t$ combine into a simple support with masses $1-u_t$ on $\{t\}$ and $u_t$ on $\Theta_p$. Combining across teams, a non-empty intersection arises when one team's focal element meets frames of the others or when $\Theta_p$ meets $\Theta_p$; two distinct singletons give the empty set. The unnormalised masses are therefore $\tilde m(\{t\})=(1-u_t)\prod_{s\ne t}u_s$ and $\tilde m(\Theta_p)=\prod_s u_s$. Dempster's normalisation divides by their sum, a common factor; dividing all by $\prod_s u_s$ gives weights $(1-u_t)/u_t=e^{\omega c_t}-1$ and $1$. $\square$

Hence $q_E(t)=\dfrac{e^{\omega c_t}-1+1/K}{1+\sum_s(e^{\omega c_s}-1)}$. A cautious conjunctive rule (canonical decomposition with the minimum of weights) was also evaluated in an early study.

**DA (similarity-based source discounting).** This is a heuristic discounting of sources that are similar to other sources assigning the same team; it does not establish independence and does not recover an unbiased effective sample size. The counts are corrected for similarity between sources: $g_i(p)=1+\#\{j\ne i:x_j(p)=x_i(p),\,s_{ij}\ge\sigma\}$, $\alpha_i(p)=1/(1+\lambda(g_i(p)-1))$, $\tilde c_t=\sum_{i:x_i(p)=t}\alpha_i(p)$, and the closed form of Proposition 1 is applied to $\tilde c$ ($\lambda=1$, $\sigma=0.7$; $s_{ij}$ is the similarity of sources $i,j$ over the patients).

**Dg and Eg (sharpened).** Any target $P$ is sharpened by the same operation
$$P^{(\gamma)}(t)=\varepsilon+(1-K\varepsilon)\,\frac{P(t)^\gamma}{\sum_{s\in\mathcal A_p}P(s)^\gamma}.$$
Dg is Dp sharpened with $\gamma=6$; Eg($\gamma$) is E sharpened with exponent $\gamma$; for $\gamma=1$ the target is not recovered exactly: it is affinely mixed with the uniform distribution, $P^{(1)}=\varepsilon\mathbf 1+(1-K\varepsilon)P$. The operation is identical for both representations, which makes the comparison symmetric.

The value $w=0.3$ was inherited from an earlier calibration on the edge of its grid and is therefore informed by hypervolume; this is declared as a limitation (Section 8).

## 4.3 Concentration: three properties

We measure the concentration of a target by its maximum $q_{\max}=\max_tq(t)$ and its entropy $H(q)=-\sum_tq(t)\ln q(t)$.

> **Proposition 2 (response to unanimity).** If all $N$ sources agree on team $t$, then $q_E(t)=1-(1-1/K)(1-w)^N$, which tends to 1 exponentially fast in $N$, whereas $q_{Dp}(t)=w+(1-w)/K$ for every $N$.
>
> *Proof.* Unanimity gives $c_t=N$, $D=e^{\omega N}$, $m(\{t\})=1-(1-w)^N$, $m(\Theta_p)=(1-w)^N$; add $m(\Theta_p)/K$. For Dp, $c_t/N=1$. $\square$

With $w=0.3$ and $K=4$: $q_E=0.98$ for $N=10$ against $q_{Dp}=0.475$. More generally, for large counts $m(\{t\})/m(\{s\})\to e^{\omega(c_t-c_s)}$: the evidential target behaves like a Boltzmann distribution on counts with inverse temperature $\omega=-\ln(1-w)\approx0.357$ (each source contributes the weight of evidence $\omega$, which adds), while Dp is affine in the counts. The evidential target is therefore *adaptively* concentrated: it sharpens as agreement among sources grows, unlike a fixed power.

> **Proposition 3 (sharpening is tempering).** Let $P$ have strictly positive components, $\gamma>0$ and $0\le\varepsilon<1/K$. (a) For $\varepsilon=0$, $P^{(\gamma)}\propto\exp(\gamma\ln P)$ is a Boltzmann distribution with inverse temperature $\gamma$ and $\dfrac{d}{d\gamma}H(P^{(\gamma)})=-\gamma\,\mathrm{Var}_{P^{(\gamma)}}(\ln P)\le0$. (b) For every $\varepsilon$, $\gamma_2>\gamma_1$ implies that $P^{(\gamma_2)}$ majorises $P^{(\gamma_1)}$, hence $H(P^{(\gamma_2)})\le H(P^{(\gamma_1)})$ and $q_{\max}$ is non-decreasing in $\gamma$. (c) $q_{\max}\le1-(K-1)\varepsilon$.
>
> *Proof.* (a) Write $P^{(\gamma)}=e^{\gamma s-A(\gamma)}$ with $s=\ln P$ and $A=\ln\sum e^{\gamma s}$. Then $H=A-\gamma E[s]$ and $A'=E[s]$, so $H'=-\gamma\,E'[s]=-\gamma\,\mathrm{Var}(s)$. (b) Let $r^{(\gamma)}=P^\gamma/\sum P^\gamma$ and order the teams so that $P_1\ge\dots\ge P_K$ (this order is the same for every $\gamma$). The partial sum $F_j(\gamma)=\sum_{i\le j}r^{(\gamma)}_i=E_\gamma[\mathbf 1_{i\le j}]$ has derivative $\mathrm{Cov}_\gamma(\mathbf 1_{i\le j},\ln P_i)\ge0$, because both functions are non-increasing in $i$ (Chebyshev's association inequality). All partial sums of the ordered vector are therefore non-decreasing in $\gamma$ and the totals are equal to 1, which is majorisation. The floored target is $q=(1-K\varepsilon)r+K\varepsilon u$ with $u$ uniform; it has the same order and partial sums $(1-K\varepsilon)F_j+K\varepsilon\,j/K$, again non-decreasing in $\gamma$, so majorisation is preserved. Shannon entropy is Schur-concave, hence $H$ is non-increasing; $q_{\max}=\varepsilon+(1-K\varepsilon)F_1$ is non-decreasing. (c) follows from $F_1\le1$. $\square$

With $\varepsilon=0.02$ and $K=4$ the plateau is $0.94$ (Figure 3a). The exponent is therefore a one-parameter concentration scale on which the evidential target (an adaptive, count-dependent sharpening) and the sharpened frequency target (a fixed power of an affine function of the counts) can be placed, but they are not the same family: a match of mean concentration does not imply a match of distributions.

## 4.4 Classical baselines

NSGA-II [26] (elitist, binary tournament on rank and crowding, uniform crossover with probability 0.9, per-gene mutation with probability $1/n_p$ to another allowed team) and MOEA/D [27] (Tchebycheff decomposition, neighbourhood 20) are run on the same instances, seeds, decoder and budget. Every evaluated solution is recorded in an archive; the output is the non-dominated set of the archive, re-evaluated in the oracle world exactly as for QGA2-E.

## 4.5 Other variants

- **Fourth objective in survival selection.** Ignorance (the mean mass on the frame), a random value, diversity, or ignorance used only as a tie-break are added to the three objectives for the non-dominated sorting of the survival step.
- **Regulation of the dependence cap.** Adaptive variants of the dependence correction driven by the mass on the frame.
- **Guided mutation in NSGA-II.** The target $q$ is computed once per generation from the first front of the parent population. When a gene is selected to mutate (probability $1/n_p$, unchanged and drawn first from the NSGA-II mutation stream), the new team is drawn from $q$ restricted to $\mathcal A_p\setminus\{x_p\}$ and renormalised, using a dedicated random stream; with $q$ uniform the procedure is bit-for-bit NSGA-II. Selection, crossover, population size, budget and elitism are unchanged. Arms: GDp, GE, GDg6, GEg6 and GEgm (an evidential target with an exponent calibrated to match the concentration of GDg6).

# 5. Experimental design and decision rules

## 5.1 Outcome, pairing and statistics

The outcome of a run is the oracle hypervolume (Section 3.3). For seed $s$ and arm $a$ let $h_{a,s}=\frac16\sum_{c=1}^6\mathrm{HV}^{\mathrm{oracle}}_{a,s,c}$ over the six scenarios. A contrast between arms $a$ and $b$ is $\Delta_{ab}=\frac1n\sum_s(h_{a,s}-h_{b,s})$ on the same seeds and instances. Intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals from resampling seeds jointly ($B=20{,}000$, deterministic random stream per analysis).

Two reporting regimes were used. The earlier studies were analysed with 95% bootstrap intervals and Holm-adjusted tests, without an equivalence margin. The later studies define a family of $m=4$ or $5$ primary contrasts with $(1-\alpha/m)$ intervals, i.e. 99% for $\alpha=0.05$, $m=5$ (for $m=4$, 99% is slightly conservative relative to 98.75%), and a practical-equivalence margin $\delta=0.005$ of hypervolume. Holm-adjusted $p$-values do not make the accompanying 95% intervals simultaneous; the two regimes do not give the same protection against multiplicity. With interval $[L,U]$ the decision for a contrast is:

- *superiority* if $L>0$; *inferiority* if $U<0$;
- *equivalence* if $[L,U]\subset[-\delta,\delta]$;
- *inconclusive* otherwise.

An interval that contains 0 does not demonstrate equivalence, and an inferiority whose interval extends beyond $-\delta$ is not an equivalence either. Secondary contrasts are reported with 95% intervals and are exploratory. The sequence of studies was not corrected for multiplicity globally (Section 8).

## 5.2 Governance

Every confirmatory result comes from a protocol written before the run, with the following steps in a fixed order: written protocol (hypotheses, contrasts, margins, decision rules, seeds); implementation with unit and integration tests (gates, e.g. exact reproduction of earlier arms, exact budget, no use of oracle values in the search, reproducibility); any calibration done without reading hypervolume; a freeze file of cryptographic hashes of protocol, code and parameters, verified before the run; the run, with results stored in atomic shards; a pre-analysis check of completeness and freeze; and one single analysis in which hypervolume is read for the first time. No run was repeated to improve a result, and no hypothesis, margin or rule was changed after results were read. Defects found in tests or checks were corrected before the freeze or, when found at the analysis check, before any hypervolume was read; each is declared in the supplementary appendix.


## 5.3 What "pre-registered" means here

New contrasts, margins and decision rules were fixed before each run. The reference results of earlier studies (for example the stored runs of E, Dp and Dg on seeds 10400–10499) had already been seen when later studies were designed, and several later studies reuse those seeds as paired references. The design is therefore sequential and adaptive across studies, and blind only within each study's own run.

## 5.4 Seeds and sample sizes

Each study uses seeds not used by calibration or technical tests, which used separate seeds. The two main seed ranges are 11200–11299 (comparison of classical algorithms with the QGA2-E variants, and the dependence-corrected evidential target) and 10400–10499 (sharpening curves and transfer to NSGA-II). Sample sizes are 100 seeds for most studies, 200 or 300 for the earliest ones, 60 for two later studies and 30 for two pilots; they are listed with each result; the dataset is described in Appendix B. The two latest studies (Section 6.6) use seeds 12000–12099 (the second reuses the first, so they are not independent replications), with calibration on 12700–12714 and 13700–13714 and technical tests on 12900–12909 and 13900–13909. The two independent evaluations (Section 6.7) use seeds 14000–14059 (new synthetic geographies; calibration 14700–14714, technical tests 14900–14909) and 15000–15059 (benchmark geographies; calibration 15700–15714, technical tests 15900–15909). The concentration-schedule study (Section 6.8) uses seeds 16000–16059 on the six benchmark geographies of the second evaluation (new seeds and new patient samples; calibration 16700–16714, technical tests 16900–16909). Contrasts are paired within a study only: two means from different seed ranges can be compared only as orders of magnitude (Appendix D).

# 6. Results

The results are organised by question. Three main findings structure them: (1) the evidential target improves QGA2-E over the simple probabilistic target (Section 6.3); (2) a sharpened probabilistic target reproduces that advantage and sharpening the evidential target does not extend it (Section 6.4); (3) the advantage does not transfer to guiding NSGA-II mutation (Section 6.9); (4) two independent evaluations on new families of instances reproduce the advantage of the evidential targets over the simple probabilistic target and the equivalence of DA and E at a high source strength, but not a stable superiority of the sharpened probabilistic target (Section 6.7). Sections 6.2, 6.5 and 6.6 are complementary investigations; the detail of the refinement studies of Section 6.5 is given in Appendix E. All differences are in oracle hypervolume; the mean hypervolume of the QGA2-E variants is about 0.5 and that of NSGA-II about 0.65. ## 6.3 Evidential against probabilistic targets in QGA2-E

The evidential construction was first compared with probabilistic controls built from the same observations (n = 200). Dempster's rule (V1) beat the matched probabilistic control (V0c) by +0.0250 [+0.0238, +0.0262]; the cautious rule (V2) was worse than both (V2 − V0c = −0.0092 [−0.0100, −0.0084]; V2 − V1 = −0.0342 [−0.0355, −0.0330]). In a follow-up with two layers of evidence (data and search model) crossed, using the nominal world and the non-informative world of Section 3.4, the effect of the evidential search layer with the data layer held evidential was +0.0200 [+0.0186, +0.0213] in the nominal world, whereas stacking evidential layers gave no additional benefit (the two layer-interaction contrasts were negative, −0.0064 and −0.0188, both significant after Holm adjustment). A fully evidential pipeline was then compared with a fully probabilistic one, the latter using the probabilistic-average world: E − P = +0.0275 [+0.0261, +0.0289], 99.5% of seeds favourable; the search layer contributed more than the data layer (+0.0205 for the evidential against the matched probabilistic search model with evidential data; +0.0106 for evidential against probabilistic data with evidential search). In the QGA2-E architecture (n = 200), E − P = +0.0459 [+0.0444, +0.0473] with 100% of seeds favourable. Cleaning dependence between sources before Dempster's rule kept the advantage but reduced it (Ec − Pc = +0.0147 [+0.0135, +0.0159]; interaction −0.0040 [−0.0049, −0.0031]; Ec − E = −0.0043).

In every study an evidential target built by Dempster's rule from the source set had a higher hypervolume than the probabilistic target of the same study; the later pairwise values are in Table 1 (E − Dp between +0.0427 and +0.0459 in the studies on the common seed ranges). Those probabilistic targets were not matched in concentration (Section 6.4), so these studies do not say whether the representation or the resulting sharpness is responsible.

## 6.4 Concentration of the target

**Diagnostic.** The target of each arm was recorded at every generation. At generation 150 the mean maximum of the target was 0.281 for Dp, 0.805 for Dg, 0.851 for E and 0.911 for Eg1.5, with entropy 1.452, 0.646, 0.525 and 0.388 (Figure 3b). The evidential target is far more concentrated than the simple probabilistic target, as predicted by Proposition 2: the accumulation of agreement among sources is exponential, whereas the frequency-based target stays affine in the counts.

**Sharpening the probabilistic target.** In a descriptive study (n = 200), the power-sharpened probabilistic target Dg ($\gamma=6$) reproduced and slightly exceeded the evidential advantage: Dg − Dp = +0.0454 [+0.0439, +0.0469], E − Dp = +0.0414, E − Dg = −0.0040 [−0.0055, −0.0025]. In the confirmatory comparison (n = 100), DA − Dp = +0.0427 [+0.0401, +0.0454] and DA − Dg = −0.0036 [−0.0064, −0.0009] (primary), an inferiority of DA with an interval that extends beyond the 0.005 margin; E − Dg = −0.0036 [−0.0058, −0.0015] (secondary, 95%). The probabilistic sharpening curve (n = 60) has an interior optimum: relative to Dg ($\gamma=6$), Dg(8) was +0.0031 [+0.0007, +0.0056] and Dg(12) was −0.0069, while Dg(2) was only +0.0045 above Dp. In the descriptive study the matching gate on concentration (relative entropy gap ≤ 10% and |Δq_max| ≤ 0.05) was not met by any exponent of the grid; $\gamma=6$ was kept as the best attempt (relative entropy gap about 40%, |Δq_max| 0.093) and declared non-conforming.

**Sharpening the evidential target.** Applying the same sharpening to the evidential target made it worse (n = 100; Table 3, Figure 5). Eg2 − E = −0.0046 [−0.0076, −0.0016] and Eg6 − E = −0.016 [−0.0188, −0.0130] with 99% intervals; Eg6 − Dg = −0.0204 [−0.0231, −0.0177]. The curve around the natural exponent (n = 100, family of four) is a plateau: Eg1.1 − E = +0.0010 [−0.0015, +0.0036], Eg1.25 − E = +0.0006 [−0.0020, +0.0031] and Eg0.9 − E = −0.0007 [−0.0031, +0.0017] were all equivalent to E within the margin; Eg0.8 − E = −0.0047 [−0.0069, −0.0025] was inferior. The nominal maximum is at $\gamma=1.5$ (Eg1.5 − E = +0.0016, 95% CI [−0.0008, +0.0040], secondary), which cannot be told apart from E. The evidential target is therefore near its optimum at its natural concentration, and the same hypervolume plateau (Figure 5) coincides with entropies between 0.39 and 0.59 at generation 150 (Figure 3b and the diagnostics recorded in the same study).

## 6.5 Refinements of the evidential structure

Four further ideas were tested, each pre-registered, to see whether the evidential structure could add something beyond a sharper target: a dependence correction (DA), ignorance as a fourth survival objective, adaptive regulation of the dependence cap, and diversity as a fourth objective. None produced a robust gain: the only positive effect (ignorance as a fourth objective, +0.0077) was matched or exceeded by a random fourth objective (−0.0042 for ignorance against the random one), the adaptive regulation was equivalent to the fixed cap in all five contrasts, and diversity as a fourth objective was inferior (−0.0094). The full list of contrasts is in Appendix E.

## 6.6 Dependence degree and local sensitivity

Two later studies examined the dependence-corrected target DA in more detail. In DA, each source is discounted according to the number of sources that are similar to it (similarity at least σ = 0.7) and support the same team; a dependence-degree parameter λ scales that discount, with λ = 0 equal to E and λ = 1 the DA of the previous sections. Both studies used the oracle hypervolume, paired seeds 12000–12099 (n = 100, six scenarios), 99% bootstrap intervals for primary contrasts and the margin δ = 0.005. They were run under the same governance (Section 5.2); the second reuses the seeds and the reference arms of the first, so the two are not independent replications.

**Dependence degree.** The first study varied λ ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 1}. For each λ, a power-sharpened probabilistic target Dg was selected on a calibration sample of other seeds (scenario subset, hypervolume not read) so that its mean entropy and maximum probability matched those of DA(λ) over generations 1–150. The selection retained γ = 6 for all three values of λ, an edge of the calibration grid; the three Dg arms are therefore the same arm and the three contrasts share a single reference. On the evaluation scenarios, the match was conforming for all three (entropy within 4.5%, maximum probability within 0.024). Mean hypervolume: E 0.5281, DA(0.25) 0.5283, DA(0.5) 0.5279, DA(1) 0.5273, Dg 0.5312. The primary contrasts were DA(λ) − Dg = −0.0029 [−0.0055, −0.0003], −0.0032 [−0.0058, −0.0007] and −0.0039 [−0.0066, −0.0014] for λ = 0.25, 0.5 and 1 (decision rule: inferiority, with the lower bounds close to the margin), and DA(1) − E = −0.0009 [−0.0025, +0.0007] (equivalence). The parameter λ changed the target markedly: at generation 150 the mean maximum probability fell from 0.845 (E) to 0.754, 0.728 and 0.709 (λ = 0.25, 0.5, 1), and its entropy rose from 0.542 to 0.753, 0.805 and 0.842, without a change of hypervolume. A continuous pairwise dependence rule was indistinguishable from E (−0.0001 [−0.0021, +0.0019], 95% interval, exploratory); a simplified mixture of the evidential and cautious targets (not the published mixed rule) was clearly worse than E (−0.0149 [−0.0171, −0.0128]) and did not match the concentration of DA (entropy gap 29%).

**Local sensitivity.** The second study varied one parameter at a time around the reference (source strength w = 0.3, σ = 0.7, γ = 6): w ∈ {0.15, 0.5, 0.7}, σ ∈ {0.6, 0.8}, and fixed exponents γ = 8 and γ = 12 at w = 0.3. For every setting where it applies, the Dg exponent was chosen on a calibration sample without reading hypervolume, and the match was then verified on the evaluation scenarios with the same thresholds (relative entropy gap ≤ 10%, gap of the maximum probability ≤ 0.05); a contrast whose match failed was not re-calibrated and is reported as *unmatched*. Sixteen contrasts were defined (intervals are marginal 99% intervals and do not control the family simultaneously). Table 5 gives the main ones.

At w = 0.15 the targets hardly concentrate: in 99% to 100% of the runs of two of the six scenarios DA and E gave exactly the same hypervolume, which is consistent with DA and E being the same algorithm when no pair of sources reaches the similarity threshold.

## 6.7 Independent evaluations on new families of instances

**Purpose.** All decisions of the earlier studies were made on scenarios derived from three Solomon geographies. Two further studies ask which of the main contrasts hold on instances that played no role in those decisions. They are not replications on the same seeds: the geographies, the sizes and the seeds are new, and the free parameter of the sharpened target was calibrated again each time. Each protocol was written before any calibration or run. In both, only the geography changes; the hidden truth and the evidence sources remain simulated, so the instances are semi-synthetic.

**First evaluation: design.** Synthetic Solomon-like geographies were generated per seed: uniform (U), clustered (K, eight Gaussian clusters) and mixed (M), with two sizes each (40 patients and 9 teams; 70 patients and 12 teams), giving six scenarios; the rest of the generator (hidden truth, evidence sources and reliabilities) and the search engine were unchanged. Sixty seeds were used per scenario (360 instances), with the same budget of 6,040 evaluations, the same oracle hypervolume and the same decision rules as before (99% marginal intervals, margin 0.005). The arms were E, Dp, DA (λ = 1, σ = 0.7) and Dg at source strengths w = 0.3 and w = 0.7, plus Dg with γ = 6 at w = 0.3. The exponent of Dg was selected without hypervolume on a calibration sample (other seeds, the three smaller scenarios) by the same rule as before: γ = 6 at w = 0.3, where no exponent of the grid satisfied the concentration gate (best attempt), and γ = 4 at w = 0.7, where the gate was satisfied. On the evaluation scenarios the match of mean concentration over generations 1–150 was not conforming at w = 0.3 (relative entropy gap 23%, difference of maximum probability 0.133) and was conforming at w = 0.7 (5.9%, 0.020).

## 6.8 Matching the concentration trajectory: representation or schedule?

**Purpose.** In the earlier comparisons the evidential target concentrates on a schedule of its own (Dempster's rule sharpens as the non-dominated individuals agree), whereas the sharpened probabilistic target Dg uses one fixed exponent for all 150 generations; a difference between them therefore mixes representation and schedule. This study replaces the fixed exponent by an exponent that varies in time, $\gamma_t=g_0+(g_1-g_0)\min\{1,(t-1)/(G_s-1)\}$ at generation $t$, applied to the simple probabilistic target and renormalised with the same floor (0.02); the resulting target is denoted Dgt. A constant ramp ($g_0=g_1$) reproduces Dg bit for bit (tested). The protocol was written before any calibration or run.

**Design.** Geographies, sizes, generator and search engine are those of the second independent evaluation (six benchmark geographies, 50 patients, 10 teams), with new seeds (16000–16059, 60 per geography, 360 instances) and new patient samples; the geographies are therefore *not* independent of the second evaluation. The schedule was selected once per source strength and per reference target (E or DA, λ = 1, σ = 0.7) without reading hypervolume, on other seeds (16700–16714; geographies X, Golden and ORTEC) from 12 candidates ($g_0\in\{1,3\}$, $g_1\in\{6,10,16\}$, $G_s\in\{30,90\}$): among the candidates that satisfied a gate on the whole trajectory (mean over generations 1–150 of the relative entropy gap ≤ 10% and of $|\Delta q_{\max}|$ ≤ 0.05), the one minimising the sum of the mean squared relative entropy gap and of the mean squared $\Delta q_{\max}/0.05$; if none satisfied the gate, the best attempt was kept and declared. The gate was satisfied in calibration for three of the four (strength, reference) pairs, and not for w = 0.7 with the DA reference. The same schedule was selected for E and for DA at each strength, so there are two Dgt arms, one per source strength. Whether the match holds was verified on the evaluation instances, without recalibration (Table 9). Twelve arms were run (E, DA, Dp, Dgt and the fixed Dg with γ = 6, at w = 0.3 and w = 0.7), with the oracle hypervolume, the budget of 6,040 evaluations and the decision rules of the other studies (per-seed mean over the six geographies, paired bootstrap B = 20,000, 99% marginal intervals, margin 0.005; the Bonferroni intervals for the five contrasts of each strength are given as a sensitivity analysis). The pre-registered reading was conditional on the conformity of the trajectory: with a conforming match, a superior E (or DA) would mean that an advantage of the representation or of the dynamics is not explained by the schedule alone; a superior Dgt, that a probabilistic schedule does better than Dempster's rule; a non-conforming match would make the contrast descriptive only.

At the high strength the conclusion was the opposite: with a conforming match, the probabilistic schedule did better than Dempster's rule. No general advantage or disadvantage of the evidential representation follows.

**Limits of the match.** The gate controls two scalar summaries of the mean concentration per generation. The effective decoded distributions of the matched arms remained far apart at generation 150 (JS between 0.17 and 0.33, and an L1 distance between 0.74 and 0.96 out of a maximum of 2), so the arms are not equivalent in distribution. The schedule family has three parameters and was selected on three geographies; the gate failed for one of the four pairs, the two Dgt arms are the same arm for the two references, the geographies are those of the second evaluation, and only one algorithm and one evidence generator are used. These results are conditional on this design.

## 6.10 Summary of the confirmatory contrasts

Figure 7 gathers the primary contrasts of the studies of Sections 6.3 to 6.9 on one scale; the dependence-degree and sensitivity studies (Section 6.6) are summarised in Table 5 and the independent evaluations (Section 6.7) in Tables 6 and 7 and the concentration-schedule study (Section 6.8) in Tables 8 and 9; none is drawn in it. Two patterns stand out in the studies of the main instances: the sharpened probabilistic target is at least as good as the evidential one (first block; this was not stable in the independent evaluations of Section 6.7), and evidential sharpening beyond $\gamma\approx1.5$ is harmful while sharpening within 0.9–1.25 is equivalent to no sharpening (second and third blocks).

# 7. Discussion

**What the evidence supports.** Within QGA2-E, an evidential target built by Dempster's rule is better than a simple probabilistic target, by 0.025 to 0.046 depending on the study. A probabilistic target sharpened by a power transformation obtains at least as much (DA − Dg = −0.0036, primary, and E − Dg = −0.0036, secondary, in the confirmatory comparison; −0.0040 in the descriptive one; Section 6.4), so the evidential target has no advantage over the best probabilistic target tested. Sharpening the evidential target does not help: the curve is flat from $\gamma=0.9$ to $\gamma=1.5$ and falls for stronger sharpening. These results are consistent with the view that the observed initial advantage is largely a matter of how concentrated the sampling distribution is, as Proposition 2 suggests: the evidential target responds to agreement exponentially, the frequency target only linearly.

**Dependence degree and sensitivity.** The dependence-degree parameter changes the concentration of the evidential target substantially, yet at the reference setting hypervolume is flat in it and the evidential target stays equivalent to E; so, in this set-up, a change of the concentration of the evidential target through that parameter is not what drives performance. Whether DA is better or worse than a concentration-matched sharpened probabilistic target depends on the setting (slightly better at w = 0.15, slightly worse at the reference and at σ = 0.8, and not interpretable where the match failed), and the evidential target remains better than the simple probabilistic target everywhere. The match of mean concentration over 150 generations is a weak notion of equivalence: trajectories, initial states and decoded distributions differ, and the exponent selected by the match was not the one that maximised hypervolume. The relative performance of DA on clustered scenarios is a hypothesis for later work, not a result.

**Independent evaluations.** On two new families of instances (Section 6.7) two findings were reproduced: the evidential targets are far better than the simple probabilistic target (+0.0203 to +0.0247 on benchmark geographies, +0.0216 on synthetic ones), and DA and E are practically equivalent at a high source strength. The relation between the evidential targets and the sharpened probabilistic target was not reproduced as a stable ordering: the sharpened target was better on the synthetic geographies, and on the benchmark geographies it was worse, better or not different from DA depending on the exponent and the source strength. The earlier small advantage of DA over E at a high source strength was reproduced on neither family. The exponent chosen by the calibration rule did not always transfer, and the concentration gate was satisfied only at w = 0.7; a practitioner who sharpens a probabilistic target should tune the exponent on instances of the target family.

**Representation or schedule.** A last pre-registered study (Section 6.8) replaced the fixed exponent of the probabilistic target by a schedule that follows the evidential trajectory of entropy and maximum probability. The result depends on the source strength: at w = 0.3 the evidential target was better than the scheduled probabilistic target (E − Dgt = +0.0066), at w = 0.7 it was worse (−0.0050), and a fixed exponent of 6 was better than both at w = 0.3. The schedule therefore does not explain away the evidential advantage at w = 0.3 in this design, but there is no consistent advantage of the representation across source strengths, and the match was on two diagnostics only (decoded distributions remained far apart); the study does not establish a representation effect independent of concentration, nor exclude it. **Possible explanation (untested hypothesis).** The trajectory match controls the mean entropy and the mean maximum probability, but the decoded distributions of the matched arms stayed far apart at generation 150 (Jensen–Shannon divergence 0.17 at w = 0.3 and 0.33 at w = 0.7, with mean relative entropy gaps of 4.8% and 5.9%). Two targets with the same entropy and the same maximum can spread the remaining probability differently over the other allowed teams, and can differ in the patients on which they concentrate first; such differences may matter more at the higher source strength, where the divergence was larger and the sign of the contrast changed. We did not test this: it would require comparing per-patient distributions and archive diversity from the stored runs, a post hoc analysis that was not part of the pre-registered protocol.

**What it does not show.** The experiments compare transformations of distributions; they do not intervene on concentration while holding everything else constant. The concentration of the evidential target is adaptive (it grows with agreement among the sources), while that of Dg is fixed by the exponent (Section 6.8 tests a scheduled exponent, but matches only two diagnostics); Proposition 3 shows that sharpening is a tempering, but the two families are not the same, and matching their mean concentration does not match their distributions. The one concentration-matched contrast (DAgm − Dg) is inconclusive rather than null. The data therefore do not support the statement "the representation never helps". They support: no representation advantage independent of concentration was established in the configurations tested. A post-hoc mechanistic analysis of stored traces (exploratory, not pre-registered) points in the same direction but remains a hypothesis.

**Why the effect does not transfer.** In QGA2-E the target is the only driver of the search: every angle is moved towards it at every generation. In NSGA-II the target only decides which new team a mutated gene receives, and with a mutation rate of $1/n_p$ this is about one gene per child. Tournament selection, crossover and elitism do the rest, and guiding the mutation by the probabilistic target did not change hypervolume (equivalence), so the room for the representation to matter was very small. This is a plausible mechanistic hypothesis, not a causal mechanism that was tested; other integration points (an additional objective, a crossover bias) were not examined.

**Why classical algorithms are better here.** NSGA-II and MOEA/D exceed every QGA2-E variant by 0.11 to 0.17. Earlier studies associated part of the gap with elitist replacement (QGA2 was within 0.017 of a non-elitist NSGA-II in an early study; Section 6.2 gives the other early comparisons), but adding elitism to QGA2 did not close it (QGA2-E − QGA2 = −0.0296). The study was not designed to explain the gap, which is the same whichever target is used. A candidate explanation that we did not test is structural: QGA2-E samples each patient independently from its own angle vector, so it cannot learn dependences between the team choices of different patients (for example, which patients should share a team to balance the workload), whereas crossover in NSGA-II and MOEA/D can preserve and recombine such combinations. Testing it would require a model with pairwise dependences, which is a different algorithm. This is why the paper is positioned as a study of target construction in a univariate model-based search rather than as a contribution to home-care optimisation.

**Practical consequences.** For a practitioner who uses a probabilistic frequency target in a QGA2-like algorithm, sharpening it with a power transformation (exponent about 6 to 8 in this set-up) recovered the benefit that an evidential target also gives on the main instances, with a simpler model, but on benchmark geographies an exponent of 4 at the reference source strength was clearly worse than the evidential target, so the exponent must be tuned; in the latest study the best exponent at w = 0.3 among those tested was 8 rather than 6. For a practitioner who already uses an evidential target, further sharpening or the refinements studied here are not worth their cost. At this budget, a classical NSGA-II or MOEA/D is a safer choice in this problem class.

**Negative and inconclusive results as results.** Several of the most informative findings are negative or inconclusive: the refinements that did not help, the concentration-matched contrast that did not conclude, and the failed transfer. We report them in full because the pre-registered sequence is what makes them interpretable.

# 8. Limitations

1. **One set-up.** Six scenarios derived from one benchmark family, a fixed visit order, a budget of 6,040 evaluations, one hypervolume reference point, and one family of uncertainty models (a single synthetic generator of evidence). No claim is made outside this set-up.
2. **Parameter $w=0.3$.** The support strength was inherited from a calibration whose optimum lay on the edge of its grid, so it is informed by hypervolume. It was not re-tuned for each arm and acts as a tuning knob of the evidential target; w was varied locally in the latest study (Section 6.6), with a clear effect on the speed of concentration of the target.
3. **Reference results already seen.** Several studies reuse seeds and stored reference runs seen in earlier studies; the sequence is adaptive across studies.
4. **Concentration matching is approximate.** The matching of entropy and $q_{\max}$ was based on means over a calibration sample (different scenario set), not on the whole distribution, and the evidential and sharpened-frequency targets are different families (Section 4.3). In one descriptive study the matching gate failed; in the transfer study the matched arm degenerated into the natural evidential arm.
5. **Multiplicity.** Each study controls its own family but there is no correction across the whole sequence.
6. **Cancelled and unexecuted studies.** One planned study was cancelled by the author's written decision and one was never executed; neither is a negative result.
7. **Sample sizes.** n = 60 for two studies limits precision; the contrast on representation is inconclusive partly for this reason.
8. **Descriptive studies.** Three studies are descriptive; their numbers are context.
9. **Implementation scope.** Evidential structure is applied up to the pignistic probability and then, for sharpened arms, transformed as a probability; some evidential properties are not preserved after the transformation. "Sharpened evidential target" means "evidential target followed by pignistic sharpening".
10. **Simplified problem.** Fixed visit order and an assignment-only decision; the results may not carry over to integrated routing and scheduling.
11. **Semi-synthetic data.** Only the customer coordinates come from public benchmark instances (artificial vehicle-routing instances, not data from a care organisation); time windows, skills, team certifications, the hidden truth and the evidence sources are generated, as is usual for published home-care benchmarks, and no real home-care case study is included. **Synthetic evidence.** The evidence sources are simulated from the hidden truth with fixed reliabilities; real HHC data may have different error structures.
12. **Local, one-at-a-time sensitivity.** In the latest study w, σ and γ were varied one at a time; their interactions were not studied, the grids are short (three values of w, two of σ, two fixed exponents) and the calibration grid stops at γ = 16. For three settings the match of concentration failed on the evaluation scenarios, and those contrasts cannot be read as effects of the representation.
13. **Dependent studies and multiplicity.** The two latest studies share seeds and reference arms; intervals of the 16 contrasts of the latest study are marginal and do not control the family; the exponent selected for the three values of the dependence degree lay at the edge of its calibration grid.
14. **Post hoc descriptive analysis.** The trajectory, tail, scenario-type and decoded-distribution observations of Section 6.6 were defined after the results had been seen, without multiplicity control; per-patient decoded frequencies exist only for the latest study.
15. **Objective function.** The time-window satisfaction has a step at the window end (Section 3.2); all results are conditional on it and a continuous penalty was not evaluated.
16. **Independent evaluations.** Both new families are semi-synthetic: the first uses synthetic geographies that mimic but are not Solomon files; the second uses customer coordinates from public benchmark files (artificial routing instances, one described as derived from a real delivery service), subsampled and rescaled, with simulated hidden truth and evidence. Each uses one algorithm, one evidence generator, 60 seeds, two source strengths and one exponent grid; the concentration gate was not satisfied at w = 0.3 in either calibration, and the match failed at that strength in both; the earlier studies had been seen when the protocols were written.
17. **Concentration-schedule study.** The schedule family has three parameters and was selected on three geographies; the trajectory match covers two scalar diagnostics and the decoded distributions remained far apart; the gate was not satisfied for one of the four pairs; the two scheduled arms are the same arm; the geographies are those of the second evaluation (new seeds and patient samples); the sign of the main contrast changed with the source strength.
18. **No real home-care data.** No real case study is included; the conclusions concern the algorithm and the targets on simulated information, not clinical or organisational practice.

# 9. Conclusion

In QGA2-E for multiobjective home health care team assignment, an evidential target built with Dempster's rule outperformed a simple probabilistic target, but a power-sharpened probabilistic target performed at least as well, further sharpening of the evidential target lowered its performance, and the one comparison designed to match concentration (by calibration, without guaranteeing identical concentration on the evaluation scenarios) was inconclusive. Refinements of the evidential structure (dependence correction, ignorance and diversity as selection objectives, adaptive regulation) gave no robust gain, and guiding the mutation of NSGA-II with the same targets neither improved NSGA-II nor favoured the evidential target. NSGA-II and MOEA/D stayed 0.11 to 0.17 hypervolume above all QGA2-E variants. The results are compatible with a substantial contribution of target concentration to the initial evidential advantage, without establishing it as the sole mechanism, and no representation advantage independent of concentration was established in the tested configurations.

Two later studies showed that varying the dependence degree of DA changes the concentration of its target without changing hypervolume, that the contrast between DA and a concentration-matched sharpened probabilistic target changes sign with the source strength (with the match failing at several settings), and that the advantage of the evidential target over the simple probabilistic target holds at every source strength tested. Matching mean concentration did not make trajectories or decoded distributions comparable, so the matched contrasts remain conditional on a weak form of matching.

Two independent evaluations on new families of instances (synthetic geographies, then geographies from public benchmark files; new seeds and fresh calibrations) reproduced the large advantage of the evidential targets over the simple probabilistic target and the equivalence of DA and E at a high source strength. They did not reproduce a stable superiority of the sharpened probabilistic target: on the benchmark geographies it was worse than DA, better than DA, or not different, depending on the exponent and the source strength. The instances remain semi-synthetic, with simulated hidden truth and evidence.

A final pre-registered study separated, as far as two concentration diagnostics allow, the representation from the concentration schedule by giving the probabilistic target an exponent that varies in time. With a conforming match, the evidential target was better than the scheduled probabilistic target at w = 0.3 and worse at w = 0.7, and a fixed exponent of 6 was better than both at w = 0.3, so the data support neither a general evidential advantage nor a general disadvantage once the schedule is taken into account; decoded distributions of the matched arms remained far apart.

Perspectives: a design that manipulates concentration while holding the representation fixed (and the reverse) with exact distributional matching; a design in which the strength of the evidential contribution adapts to the state of the search (a hypothesis suggested by the strong dependence of the speed of concentration on the source strength, not tested here); other integration points of evidential information in classical algorithms (an additional objective, crossover bias); other budgets and instance families; real or semi-real evidence data; and the integrated routing and scheduling problem.

**Generated data.** For each scenario and seed the generator draws: for each patient a time window (earliest start uniform up to 70% of the day, window length 20 to 80 time units, capped by the day end minus the visit duration), exactly one required skill among five, and with probability 0.5 a preferred team; for each team a certification matrix with two or three certified skills among five, a skill level in {0, 1, 2} on each certified skill, and a maximum route length drawn between 50% and 100% of the day; and, for satisfaction, three skill groups ({0,1}, {2,3}, {4}). Then the hidden truth and the three evidence sources are generated as described in Section 3.4. Distances are Euclidean between the coordinates; the visit order within a team is fixed. A repair step gives team 0 the skills of any patient that no team can serve (Section 3.4). (These values were checked against the generator code; they differ in one place from v0.4, where skill levels were written as {0,…,3}: the code draws 0, 1 or 2. Windows: the earliest start is drawn below min(70% of the day, day end − visit − 20) and the length between 20 and 79.)

**Seeds and sample sizes.** An instance is identified by (scenario, seed); the pseudo-random streams are derived deterministically from the scenario, the seed and a stream name. The concentration-schedule study (Section 6.8) uses seeds 16000–16059 on the six geographies of Table B2 (calibration 16700–16714, technical tests 16900–16909); its additional arm Dgt is defined in Section 6.8. Three main seed ranges are used: 11200–11299, 10400–10499 and 12000–12099 (100 seeds each, 600 instances each over the six scenarios), one range for the first independent evaluation, 14000–14059 (60 seeds, 360 instances over six new scenarios V6-U, V7-U, V6-K, V7-K, V6-M and V7-M; coordinates in [0, 100]², depot first, generated per seed: uniform, eight Gaussian clusters with standard deviation 5, or half clustered and half uniform), and one range for the second, 15000–15059 (60 seeds, 360 instances over six benchmark geographies, Table B2). Earlier studies used other ranges; calibration and technical tests used separate seeds that are never used in a confirmatory analysis.

**What is stored.** For each run (scenario, seed, arm): the oracle hypervolume, the archive, and diagnostics (target concentration at selected generations, mutation counts). The final analysis of each study is one file of paired contrasts and means; the traceability of each number of the paper to its source file is given in a separate supplementary table. The code of the instance generator is a shared library whose hash is recorded in the freeze files.

**Availability.** The frozen protocols, the code, the parameter and freeze records, the final reports and the final analysis files of all studies reported here, together with both versions of this manuscript, are archived in release V1.0 of the repository https://github.com/MontZ26/Evidential_QGA2E_paper_archive (permanent link: https://github.com/MontZ26/Evidential_QGA2E_paper_archive/releases/tag/V1.0). The raw per-run result files are not included because of their size; they can be regenerated with the code and the seeds stated in each protocol. A public repository for the earlier manuscript contains the generator of that paper, the RC101 file and the raw results of its 20-seed experiments.

# Appendix D. Glossary of abbreviations

**Algorithms and variants.** GA: genetic algorithm. QGA: quantum-inspired GA, in which each gene has an angle θ and the probability of choosing a team is sin²θ. QGA2-E: the QGA of this paper, with two amplitudes (no third amplitude of ignorance) and a target computed from the population. NSGA-II: classical multiobjective GA with non-dominated sorting and crowding distance. MOEA/D: classical multiobjective GA based on decomposition into scalar sub-problems.

**Targets.** q: target probability for each team, computed from the first non-dominated front. Dp: simple probabilistic target, q = w·c_t/N + (1−w)/K. E: evidential target, Dempster's rule on simple supports followed by the pignistic probability (BetP). DA: evidential target with source discounting by similarity (dependence correction). Dg: Dp sharpened by the power γ (γ = 6 unless stated otherwise). Eg(γ) (Eg0.8, Eg1.1, Eg1.25, Eg1.5, Eg2, Eg4, Eg6): E sharpened or softened with exponent γ. DAg6, DAgm: DA sharpened with γ = 6, or with a matched exponent γ_m chosen to equalise concentration. γ_m: matched exponent (1.5 for DA in the concentration-matched comparison; 1.0 in the transfer study).

**Scenarios of Sections 6.7 and 6.8.** B-X, B-M, B-G, B-L, B-O, B-H: benchmark geographies of Table B2. V6, V7: 40 patients and 9 teams, 70 patients and 12 teams; U, K, M: uniform, clustered and mixed synthetic geography.

**Targets of Section 6.8.** Dgt: the simple probabilistic target raised to a time-varying exponent $\gamma_t$ (linear ramp from $g_0$ to $g_1$ over $G_s$ generations, then constant), renormalised with the floor 0.02; ξ: a world of adequacies and satisfactions (Section 3.1); ω: weight of evidence.

**Targets of Section 6.6.** DA(λ): DA with dependence degree λ (λ = 0 is E; λ = 1 is the DA of the other sections). σ: similarity threshold above which two sources are treated as dependent (0.7 unless stated). Dg(γ): Dp sharpened by the exponent γ, selected on a calibration sample to match the mean entropy and maximum probability of the compared target. Matched / unmatched: whether that match met the thresholds on the evaluation scenarios. JS: Jensen–Shannon divergence (base 2) between decoded distributions. P10, CVaR10: 10th percentile and mean of the lowest 10% of the hypervolumes per seed.

**Selection variants (Section 6.5).** SEL4: ignorance used as a fourth objective in the non-dominated sorting. SELW: weighted score on normalised ranks (fitness rank and ignorance rank, λ = 0.5). SELL: lexicographic order, ignorance used only to break ties. SELR: control with the same mechanism as SEL4 but a random score. DIV: diversity used as a fourth objective. R1, S: adaptive regulation of the dependence cap by ignorance, and the fixed low-intensity level of that cap.

**Early evidential studies (Section 6.3).** V1: Dempster's rule; V2: cautious rule; V0c: matched probabilistic control; Ec, Pc: evidential and probabilistic versions with dependence cleaning (c = cleaned), compared with the uncleaned E; P: fully probabilistic pipeline (probabilistic-average world); X: evidential search on probabilistic data.

**Transfer to NSGA-II.** GDp, GE, GDg6, GEg6, GEgm: NSGA-II whose mutation is guided by Dp, E, Dg (γ = 6), Eg6 or E with the matched exponent γ_m. GEgm is identical to GE in that study.

**Measures and statistics.** HV: hypervolume of the front. Oracle HV: HV after re-evaluating the archive with the hidden truth, reference point 1.2. CI: percentile bootstrap confidence interval, paired by seed. δ: equivalence margin, 0.005. Superiority, inferiority, equivalence and inconclusive: decision categories of Section 5.1. BetP: pignistic probability, m(t) + m(Θ)/K. m, Θ: belief mass and frame of discernment (total ignorance). w, ω, ε: support strength, evidential weight ω = −ln(1−w), probability floor. q_max: maximum of the target. n: number of seeds.

**Problem.** HHC: home health care. SC6: the six scenarios, profiles S4 and S5 crossed with the instances RC101, R101 and C101 (Solomon family). f1, f2, f3: the three objectives (coverage, quality of service, workload balance). N_T: number of teams. K: number of candidate teams for a patient. n_p: number of patients. M: feasibility mask.

# Appendix E. Refinement studies of Section 6.5

Four further ideas were tested to see whether the evidential structure could add something beyond a sharper target. None produced a robust gain.

- **Dependence correction (DA, pilot, n = 30, descriptive).** DA − E = −0.0003 and DA − Dg = −0.0055; followed by the confirmatory comparison above.
- **Ignorance as a fourth objective in survival selection (n = 100).** DA+SEL4 − DA = +0.0077 [+0.0049, +0.0107], but a random fourth objective did better (DA+SEL4 − DA+SELR = −0.0042 [−0.0070, −0.0015]; in the diversity study, DA+SELR − DA = +0.0116 [+0.0093, +0.0139], secondary), so the gain is not explained by ignorance.
- **Adaptive regulation of the dependence cap (n = 60).** The five pre-registered contrasts were all equivalent (for example R1 − S = 0.0000 [−0.0008, +0.0008]; S − DA = −0.0013 [−0.0030, +0.0005]).
- **Diversity as a fourth objective (n = 60).** DIV − DA = −0.0094 [−0.0129, −0.0060], inferior; ignorance used only as a tie-break (SELL − DA = −0.0002 [−0.0033, +0.0029]) and ignorance selection on the probabilistic target (Dp+SELW − Dp = −0.0004 [−0.0024, +0.0015]) were equivalent to no change.

A descriptive diagnostic (hypervolume not read) showed that a strong dependence cap reduces the mass on the frame, the premise of the regulation tested above.
